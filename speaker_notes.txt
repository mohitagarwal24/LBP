SLIDE 3 — MOTIVATION (~1.5 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────
The Indian derivatives market, particularly NIFTY 50 index options traded on
NSE, is among the most liquid in the world by contract volume. Yet the dominant
model used by practitioners — Black-Scholes — makes assumptions that
systematically fail during the kind of volatile episodes that characterize
emerging markets like India.

The key assumption violations are: constant volatility, continuous price paths,
and Gaussian log-returns. Real markets have fat tails, negative skewness, and
sudden large jumps — think of the COVID crash in March 2020 when NIFTY fell
13% in a single session, or the election surprise in June 2024 when it fell 8%
intraday.

Our project builds a model hierarchy: starting from Black-Scholes, we add jumps
to get Merton Jump-Diffusion, then stochastic volatility for Heston, and
finally combine both for the Bates model. Each step relaxes a specific BS
assumption and we quantify the improvement rigorously.

SLIDE 5 — BLACK-SCHOLES THEORY (~2 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────────────
The Black-Scholes model assumes the asset price follows Geometric Brownian
Motion: dS = mu S dt + sigma S dW(t), where mu is the drift and sigma is
constant volatility.

Applying Ito's Lemma — and this is important — the log-price follows a normal
distribution. The Ito correction term mu minus sigma-squared-over-two appears
because variance contributes a compounding drag.

For estimation, the daily log-returns are normally distributed, and we
construct the log-likelihood function. We use Maximum Likelihood Estimation
following Ait-Sahalia (2002), who showed that for the GBM case, the
discretization error vanishes as delta-t goes to zero.

For pricing, we switch from the physical measure P to the risk-neutral measure
Q via Girsanov's theorem. Under Q, the drift becomes the risk-free rate.
The celebrated Black-Scholes formula gives us the European call price as
S-zero times N-of-d1 minus K-discounted times N-of-d2. We verified put-call
parity as an internal consistency check.


SLIDE 6 — BS RESULTS (~1 minute)
─────────────────────────────────
Here we see the Monte Carlo simulation results for Black-Scholes. On the left
are sample GBM paths — notice how smooth they are, no jumps, no sudden
dislocations. The right panel shows the terminal price distribution, which is
log-normal as expected.

The key validation: our Monte Carlo price converges to the closed-form
Black-Scholes price as we increase the number of paths. The standard error
shrinks at rate one-over-square-root-of-N by the Central Limit Theorem.
This confirms both our implementation is correct and the theoretical link
between the SDE and the pricing formula holds.


SLIDE 7 — MERTON JD THEORY (~2 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────────
Now we address the main limitation of Black-Scholes by introducing the
Merton Jump-Diffusion model from 1976. The key innovation: we superimpose a
compound Poisson jump process onto the continuous GBM diffusion.

The SDE now has three components: the drift term, the diffusion (Brownian)
term, and a jump term where N(t) is a Poisson process with intensity lambda
and each jump size J follows a normal distribution.

The critical result: conditional on k jumps occurring, the log-return is still
Gaussian, but with adjusted mean and variance. Marginalizing over the Poisson
distribution of jump counts gives us a mixture of Gaussians — this is what
creates the fat tails.

The variance decomposition is illuminating: total variance equals the diffusion
variance sigma-squared-delta-t PLUS the jump variance lambda-delta-t times
mu-J-squared plus sigma-J-squared. The jump component directly adds excess
kurtosis proportional to lambda, which is zero in BS.

For risk-neutral pricing, we introduce the compensated drift using kappa
equals E[e^J minus 1], ensuring the discounted price process is a martingale
under Q. The parameter vector has 5 parameters versus BS's 2, and we estimate
them via MLE using the Gaussian mixture likelihood, optimized with L-BFGS-B.


SLIDE 8 — GBM vs MJD PATHS (~45 seconds)
─────────────────────────────────────────
This figure dramatically illustrates the difference. On the left, GBM paths
are smooth and continuous. On the right, MJD paths have sudden jumps — marked
with red dots. These jumps are exactly the kind of price dislocations we
observe in real markets.

When NIFTY crashed 13% on March 23rd, 2020 — that was essentially a jump
event. GBM assigns virtually zero probability to such a move; MJD naturally
models it through its Poisson jump component.


SLIDE 9 — TAIL BEHAVIOR (~1.5 minutes)
───────────────────────────────────────
This is one of the most important slides. We compare the simulated return
distributions from BS and MJD against actual empirical NIFTY returns.

The left panel shows the full distributions — the gray is empirical, blue is
BS (Gaussian), and orange is MJD (mixture). The middle panel zooms into the
LEFT TAIL — this is the crash risk region. You can clearly see that BS has
virtually no density where the empirical distribution still has significant
mass. MJD's mixture distribution captures these fat tails much better.

The right panel uses kernel density estimation for a smooth comparison.
The empirical density has sharper peak and heavier tails than BS — a classic
sign of leptokurtosis. MJD's KDE curve tracks the empirical density much
more closely, especially in the critical tail regions.

This matters enormously for option pricing: deep out-of-the-money puts
protect against crashes. If your model underestimates crash probability, you
will systematically underprice these protective options.

On the right, empirical versus MJD quantiles — much better alignment,

SLIDE 11 — STATISTICAL MODEL SELECTION (~1 minute)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────
We don't just rely on visual comparisons — we use formal statistical criteria.
The three bar charts show log-likelihood, AIC, and BIC for both models.

MJD has strictly higher log-likelihood. The Likelihood Ratio Test statistic is
2 times the difference, distributed as chi-squared with 3 degrees of freedom
under the null hypothesis that lambda equals zero (i.e., no jumps). The
p-value is extremely small, meaning adding jumps is statistically highly
significant.

Both AIC and BIC favor MJD despite the complexity penalty for the 3 extra
parameters. This means the gain in fit from adding jumps far outweighs the
cost of additional model complexity.


SLIDE 12 — IMPLIED VOLATILITY SMILE (~1 minute)
────────────────────────────────────────────────
This is a fundamental result. We price European calls under MJD across a range
of strikes, then back out the Black-Scholes implied volatility from each MJD
price. The resulting curve is the orange line — it shows a clear volatility
smile, higher at the wings.

The blue dashed line is BS's constant sigma. BS cannot produce this pattern by
construction — it assumes flat IV across all strikes. But the market clearly
displays a smile, particularly a skew with higher IV for out-of-the-money
puts.

This smile inconsistency was first documented by Rubinstein in 1994 and is
one of the main motivations for going beyond Black-Scholes. MJD's jump
component naturally generates this skew because jumps are more impactful for
out-of-the-money options.


SLIDE 13 — PARAMETER SENSITIVITY (~45 seconds)
───────────────────────────────────────────────
This heatmap shows MJD ATM call prices as a function of two key parameters:
jump intensity lambda on the x-axis and jump volatility sigma-J on the y-axis.

The interaction is clearly non-linear — higher lambda and higher sigma-J both
increase the option price, but their combined effect is more than additive.
This represents the crash risk premium that practitioners need to capture.

This kind of sensitivity analysis is impossible in the BS world where there
are only two parameters.

SLIDE 17 — VaR BACKTESTING (~1 minute)
───────────────────────────────────────
We backtest the 1% daily Value-at-Risk using a 252-day rolling window. The
top panel shows actual returns in gray, with the BS VaR threshold in blue.
Red dots mark VaR breaches — days when the actual loss exceeded the model's
predicted worst-case.

The bottom panel shows cumulative breaches over time. The expected count for

SLIDE 18 — VARIANCE REDUCTION (~30 seconds)
────────────────────────────────────────────
Since Monte Carlo is our primary pricing engine, we implement two standard
variance reduction techniques from Glasserman (2003).

Antithetic variates pair each random draw Z with minus Z, roughly halving the
variance. Control variates use the BS closed-form as a control for the MJD
Monte Carlo, exploiting the correlation between payoffs. Both significantly
reduce standard error, which means we need fewer paths for the same accuracy.


SLIDE 19 — HESTON MODEL (~1.5 minutes)
───────────────────────────────────────
Moving to the end-term proposal. The Heston model from 1993 allows the
variance itself to be stochastic, following a CIR process. The price depends
on square-root of v(t), and the variance mean-reverts toward a long-run level
theta at speed kappa.

The key parameter rho — the correlation between price and variance innovations
— is typically negative. This captures the leverage effect: when prices fall,
volatility tends to rise.

The Feller condition 2-kappa-theta > xi-squared ensures variance stays
positive. When violated, the Euler scheme can produce negative variance —
which is where Professor Kumar's research on tamed Euler schemes becomes
directly relevant.

On the right you can see sample paths — the price paths in the top panel,
and the stochastic volatility in the bottom. Notice how volatility fluctuates
and mean-reverts, unlike the constant sigma in BS.


SLIDE 20 — HESTON IV SURFACE (~45 seconds)
───────────────────────────────────────────
This implied volatility surface shows IV as a function of moneyness and
maturity under the Heston model. The contour plot reveals the characteristic
skew and term structure — short-maturity options show a steeper smile, while
longer-maturity smiles are flatter.

This is what BS cannot produce — it would just be a flat plane at constant
sigma. The Heston model's stochastic volatility and negative correlation
naturally generate this realistic surface that matches market observations.


SLIDE 21 — SUPERVISOR'S RESEARCH (~1.5 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────────────────
This is an important connection. The CIR variance process in Heston has
a square-root diffusion coefficient — this is a non-globally-Lipschitz
coefficient. Standard Euler-Maruyama discretization can produce negative
variance values, which is mathematically inconsistent.

Professor Kumar's research directly addresses this challenge. The tamed
Euler scheme from the 2016 SIAM paper, co-authored with Dareiotis and
Sabanis, introduces a taming factor that prevents numerical explosion while
maintaining convergence. This is published in SIAM Journal on Numerical
Analysis with an impact factor of 2.712.

The explicit tamed Milstein schemes from 2017 and 2020 provide higher-order
convergence for Levy-driven SDEs with super-linear coefficients — exactly
the class of equations arising in stochastic volatility models. The regime-
switching Milstein scheme from 2020 is relevant to extensions of Heston
where volatility dynamics change across market regimes.

The McKean-Vlasov preprint represents future scope: mean-field game
formulations of multi-agent option markets.


SLIDE 22 — TAMED EULER RESULTS (~45 seconds)
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Here we demonstrate the practical impact. The left panel compares CIR
variance paths under standard Euler (blue) and tamed Euler (orange) with
deliberately high vol-of-vol to stress the scheme. Standard Euler can produce
negative excursions; tamed Euler maintains non-negativity.

The right panel shows weak convergence — the error in the expected terminal
variance versus step size. Both achieve the theoretical O(square-root-delta-t)
rate, but the tamed scheme does so without the numerical artifacts.
