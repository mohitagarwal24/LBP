"Good morning/afternoon. Today I'm presenting my mid-term evaluation on the
comparative study of stochastic models for OPTION PRICING on the NIFTY 50
index. This work is supervised by Professor Chaman Kumar.

The central question is: how well does the Black–Scholes model price European
options, and can the Merton Jump-Diffusion model do better?"

SLIDE 2: OUTLINE
-----------------
"I’ll start with motivation and data. Then I’ll cover Black–Scholes: the GBM
assumption, the Nobel insight of delta hedging leading to the BS PDE, and then
MLE calibration and the closed-form option formula. Next I’ll cover Merton
Jump-Diffusion: the SDE, the risk-neutral compensator, the series pricing
formula using the law of total expectation, and MLE for jump parameters.

Finally, I’ll present the option price comparisons across strikes/maturities,
the implied-volatility smile, crisis-event pricing, model selection criteria,
and future work."

SLIDE 3: MOTIVATION
-------------------
"NIFTY 50 options are among the most traded contracts globally. Practitioners
use Black–Scholes to price and hedge, but BS systematically misprices out-of-
the-money puts — it prices crash protection too cheaply.

The reason is the Gaussian assumption: BS assigns near-zero probability to
large crashes like COVID. The volatility smile/skew in market data is the
empirical signature of this mismatch.

Our project: calibrate BS and Merton JD on NIFTY data, then compare the implied
OPTION PRICES — where the differences matter most for OTM puts."

SLIDE 4: CALIBRATION DATA
--------------------------
"We use NIFTY 50 daily closes (2018–2024). This includes IL&FS, COVID, the
Russia–Ukraine shock, and the 2024 election surprise.

The key panels: return histogram vs Normal, and the left-tail zoom. The Normal
curve severely underestimates the frequency of extreme negative returns.

Those tail events are exactly what puts are “buying.” Underestimating them
means underpricing puts."

SLIDE 5: BS SDE & ITÔ
-----------------------
"Black–Scholes assumes GBM: dS = μSdt + σSdW.
Apply Itô to log(S) → log-price has drift (μ − σ²/2)dt and diffusion σ dW.
So log-returns are i.i.d. Gaussian and S(T) is lognormal.

Key option-pricing consequence: lognormal tails are too thin → deep OTM puts
become almost worthless under BS."

SLIDE 6: BLACK–SCHOLES PDE (DELTA-HEDGING PROOF)
-------------------------------------------------
"This is the Nobel idea: eliminate randomness by replication.

Let option value be V(S,t). Create a self-financing portfolio:
Π = V − ΔS.

Use Itô’s lemma for dV and substitute dS. In dΠ, the dW term is:
σS (V_S − Δ) dW.

Choose Δ = V_S (delta hedging). Then dW disappears — portfolio is locally
riskless over dt.

Crucial: μ cancels too. Option pricing does not require the stock’s expected
return.

No-arbitrage: riskless Π must earn r_f:
dΠ = r_f Π dt → rearrange → Black–Scholes PDE:
V_t + (1/2)σ²S²V_SS + r_f S V_S − r_f V = 0."

SLIDE 7: BS MLE (CALIBRATION)
------------------------------
"Given daily log-returns, BS/GBM implies a Normal transition density.
So the log-likelihood is explicit and MLE essentially estimates the sample mean
and variance (annualized to get σ̂).

For pricing, we move to the risk-neutral measure Q. Under Q, drift becomes r_f,
but σ stays the same. That’s why σ̂ from historical data is the key input."

SLIDE 8: MLE FOR DISCRETELY SAMPLED SDES (AÏT‑SAHALIA + IACUS)
--------------------------------------------------------------
"This slide justifies why we talk about likelihood approximations.

With discrete data, exact MLE uses the transition density:
ℓ_n(θ) = Σ log p(Δ, x_i | x_{i−1}; θ).

Aït‑Sahalia’s core point: for discretely sampled diffusions, p(Δ,·|·;θ) is
usually not available in closed form, so he constructs a convergent closed-form
approximation using Hermite polynomials.

Iacus frames this as the fixed-Δ observation scheme and notes that for discrete
data the likelihood is almost always unavailable, motivating pseudo/approximated
likelihood methods."

SLIDE 9: BS CLOSED-FORM OPTION PRICE
-------------------------------------
"Solving the BS PDE with terminal payoff gives the closed-form formula for
European call and put (via put–call parity).

d1 and d2 encode moneyness, time, and volatility. N(d2) can be interpreted as
the risk-neutral probability of finishing in-the-money.

Limit: single constant σ ⇒ BS cannot produce a volatility smile/skew."

SLIDE 10: MJD SDE
------------------
"Merton JD adds jumps to GBM: a compound Poisson process.
Jumps arrive with intensity λ; jump sizes J are Normal in log space.

Returns become diffusion + jump component. Conditional on k jumps, returns are
Gaussian with shifted mean and larger variance.

This produces a mixture distribution with fat tails — exactly what options
care about."

SLIDE 11: MERTON JD OPTION PRICING (COMPENSATOR + SERIES)
---------------------------------------------------------
"First define κ = E[e^J − 1]. Jumps add expected growth, so under risk-neutral Q
we subtract λκ from the drift to force total expected growth to equal r_f.

Then price via:
C0 = e^{−r_f T} E^Q[(S_T − K)^+].

Use the Law of Total Expectation by conditioning on N(T)=k:
C0 = Σ P(N(T)=k) * e^{−r_f T} E^Q[(S_T − K)^+ | N(T)=k].

Conditional on k, the terminal distribution is lognormal with modified (r_k, σ_k),
so the conditional price is exactly a Black–Scholes price. Therefore the final
price is a Poisson-weighted series of BS prices."

SLIDE 12: SERIES TRUNCATION — VISUAL PROOF
-------------------------------------------
"This figure makes the truncation argument precise.

Poisson weights P(N(T)=k) decay extremely fast because of k! in the denominator.
The cumulative mass approaches 1 quickly, the series price stabilizes, and the
remaining tail mass becomes tiny (log scale).

So truncating around k≈20 terms is computationally safe in our parameter regime."

SLIDE 13: MLE IN PRACTICE (IACUS) — EXACT VS PSEUDO/APPROX
-----------------------------------------------------------
"Iacus classifies estimation methods for discretely observed diffusions:
• Exact likelihood (rare, e.g., GBM/BS),
• Pseudo-likelihood (Euler Gaussian approximation),
• Approximated likelihood (Kessler, simulated likelihood, Hermite expansion).

Euler pseudo-likelihood approximates the increment as locally Gaussian over Δ.
In our project:
• BS: explicit Gaussian log-return transition ⇒ MLE is easy.
• MJD: Poisson–Gaussian mixture ⇒ MLE is numerical and bounded."

SLIDE 14: MJD LIKELIHOOD & MOMENTS
-----------------------------------
"MJD daily return density is a Poisson-weighted Gaussian mixture. We truncate
at K terms (practically sufficient). We compute the log-likelihood using
log-sum-exp for numerical stability and maximize with L-BFGS-B.

Variance decomposes into diffusion variance + jump variance. Jumps add excess
kurtosis → fat tails → higher OTM put prices."

SLIDE 15: ATM OPTION PRICES (BS VS MJD)
----------------------------------------
"At-the-money, the two models are closer because pricing is dominated by the
central part of S(T). Calls are similar; puts are slightly higher under MJD.

The key is the payoff distribution: MJD assigns more weight to large put payoffs
because it allows crashes."

SLIDE 16: OPTIONS ACROSS STRIKES
---------------------------------
"Core result: calls are similar across strikes, but puts diverge strongly for
OTM (K/S0 < 1). MJD puts are substantially higher — BS underprices crash protection."

SLIDE 17: IMPLIED VOLATILITY SMILE
-----------------------------------

BS can only give a flat line at σ̂. This connects directly to what traders see."

SLIDE 18: OTM PUT PRICING (WHERE BS FAILS MOST)
-----------------------------------------------
"Deep OTM puts: MJD/BS price ratio can be multiple times. This quantifies how

SLIDE 19: OPTION TERM STRUCTURE

SLIDE 20: PARAMETER SENSITIVITY (λ, σ_J)

SLIDE 2: OUTLINE

SLIDE 3: MOTIVATION
-------------------
"NIFTY 50 options are among the most traded contracts globally. Practitioners
use Black-Scholes to price and hedge these options. But there's a known problem:
BS systematically misprices out-of-the-money puts — it prices crash protection
too cheaply.

This happens because BS assumes Gaussian log-returns with thin tails. It assigns
near-zero probability to events like the COVID crash of -13% in a single day,
or the 2018 IL&FS crisis. But these events DO happen, and when they do, put
options pay off significantly.

The volatility smile in market data — where OTM puts trade at higher implied
volatility than ATM options — directly contradicts BS's flat-IV assumption.

Our project: calibrate BS and Merton JD to NIFTY data, price European options
under both models, and quantify how much BS underprices, especially for
protective puts."

SLIDE 4: CALIBRATION DATA
--------------------------
"We use NIFTY 50 daily closing prices from 2018 to 2024. This period includes
major events: IL&FS collapse, COVID crash, Russia-Ukraine war, and the 2024
election surprise.

The top panels show the price series and daily returns — note the occasional
extreme returns that standard models struggle with.

The bottom-left shows the return distribution versus a Normal fit. You can see
the empirical distribution has a sharper peak and fatter tails.

The bottom-right is crucial: this zooms into the LEFT TAIL, below -1%. The
red Normal curve significantly underestimates the frequency of large negative
returns. This is exactly the region where put options pay off. If your model
underestimates these probabilities, it UNDERPRICES puts."

SLIDE 5: BS SDE & ITO
-----------------------
"The Black-Scholes model assumes the underlying follows Geometric Brownian
Motion. The SDE is dS = mu*S*dt + sigma*S*dW, where W is Brownian motion.

Applying Ito's lemma to log(S), we get the log-price SDE with constant
coefficients. Integrating gives the terminal price as S(0) times an exponential
of a Normal random variable.

This means daily log-returns are iid Gaussian: r_t is Normal with mean
(mu - sigma^2/2)*dt and variance sigma^2*dt. This is what we estimate via MLE.

The key point for option pricing: the terminal S(T) is LOG-NORMAL, which has
thin tails. The probability of S(T) being far below the strike K decays very
rapidly — making deep OTM puts nearly worthless under BS."

SLIDE 6: BS MLE
-----------------
"MLE for GBM is straightforward: since log-returns are iid Normal, the
log-likelihood has a well-known form. We can derive closed-form estimators,
but we use numerical optimisation to be consistent with the MJD procedure.

The key output is sigma-hat, the estimated annualised volatility, approximately
17-18% for NIFTY. This is what enters the BS option pricing formula.

For option pricing, we switch from the real-world P-measure to the risk-neutral
Q-measure using Girsanov's theorem. Under Q, the drift becomes the risk-free
rate r_f, but critically, SIGMA STAYS THE SAME. This is why estimating sigma
from historical data is sufficient for pricing."

SLIDE 7: BS OPTION FORMULA
----------------------------
"The celebrated BS formula gives exact European call and put prices. The key
quantities are d1 and d2, which encode moneyness, time to expiry, and
volatility. N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money.

We validate using Monte Carlo: simulate N paths under Q, compute discounted
payoffs, and average. The MC estimate converges to the closed-form, confirming
our implementation.

The CRITICAL LIMITATION: sigma is constant across all strikes. This means
the implied volatility is flat — the same for every option. In market data,
we observe a smile or skew. BS fundamentally cannot reproduce this. This is
the motivation for the jump-diffusion model."

SLIDE 8: MJD SDE
------------------
"The Merton Jump-Diffusion augments GBM with a compound Poisson process.
On top of the continuous diffusion dS = mu*S*dt + sigma*S*dW, we add random
jumps: when a Poisson event occurs (rate lambda per year), the log-price jumps
by J, drawn from Normal(mu_J, sigma_J^2).

Applying the generalised Ito formula for jump-diffusions, the daily return
becomes a Gaussian part PLUS a sum of random jumps. Conditional on k jumps
occurring, the return is Normal with shifted mean and inflated variance.

This directly explains why MJD produces different option prices: the terminal
distribution S(T) is a MIXTURE of log-normals. Each jump count k produces
a different component, and the mixture has fatter tails than any single
log-normal."

SLIDE 9: MJD LIKELIHOOD & MOMENTS
-----------------------------------
"The density of daily returns under MJD is a Poisson-weighted sum of Gaussians.
We truncate at K=15 terms since the Poisson tail is negligible for lambda
below 50.

The log-likelihood sums ln of these mixture densities over all observations.
We use the log-sum-exp trick for numerical stability. Optimisation is via
L-BFGS-B with bounds ensuring sigma > 0 and lambda >= 0.

The variance decomposition is illuminating: total variance = diffusion variance
+ jump variance. The jump component lambda*(mu_J^2 + sigma_J^2) adds
ADDITIONAL uncertainty beyond what sigma alone captures.

The excess kurtosis formula shows precisely why MJD has fat tails: it's positive
whenever lambda > 0 and sigma_J > 0. And excess kurtosis is the EXACT mechanism
by which OTM put prices increase — fatter tails mean more probability of
extreme downward moves."

SLIDE 10: ATM OPTION PRICES
-----------------------------
"For at-the-money options, the two models agree reasonably well. The call prices
are very similar because the upper tail of S(T) is similar between models.

The puts show a small but consistent difference: MJD puts are slightly more
expensive. This is visible in the terminal S(T) distribution — MJD has heavier
left tail, which is what puts pay on.

The put payoff distribution on the right is the key: under MJD, there's more
weight on LARGE payoffs. These come from jump events that push S(T) well below K."

SLIDE 11: OPTIONS ACROSS STRIKES
----------------------------------
"This is the CORE RESULT. For calls, both models give similar prices across

But for PUTS, especially out-of-the-money puts where K/S0 < 1, the MJD price

The bottom-right panel shows the percentage difference. This is economically

SLIDE 13: OTM PUT PRICING

SLIDE 14: OPTION TERM STRUCTURE
---------------------------------
"For ATM options across different maturities from 7 days to 6 months, MJD puts
are consistently more expensive than BS puts.

The relative difference is LARGER for shorter maturities. This makes intuitive

longer maturities, many jumps aggregate and the central limit theorem kicks in,
making the distribution closer to Gaussian."

SLIDE 15: PARAMETER SENSITIVITY

"The heatmap shows how option prices vary with the two key jump parameters:

Higher lambda means more frequent jumps — option prices increase monotonically.
Higher sigma_J means larger jumps — prices increase faster. The interaction is
NONLINEAR: the combined effect of high lambda and high sigma_J is more than
the sum of individual effects.

This shows that accurate estimation of jump parameters is critical for reliable
option pricing."

SLIDE 17: EVENT OPTION PRICING

"This is the practical backtesting. Before each crisis — IL&FS, COVID,

This is the strongest practical argument for MJD: when crises actually happen,

SLIDE 18: VARIANCE REDUCTION
------------------------------
"Since MJD pricing requires Monte Carlo, reducing standard error matters for
practical implementation. We compare three approaches:

Naive MC has the highest standard error. Antithetic variates, which use Z and -Z

the underlying.

Control variates use the BS closed-form price as a 'control' — since we know
the BS answer exactly, we can use it to reduce noise in the MJD estimate. This
gives the best reduction, roughly 50% lower SE.

These techniques are essential for practical MJD option pricing where each
evaluation needs to be precise."
