  MATHEMATICAL EXPLANATION — STOCHASTIC OPTION PRICING PROJECT
  (Black–Scholes vs Merton Jump–Diffusion on NIFTY 50)

This file gives a deeper, equation‑level explanation of the material that
appears on each slide of the updated presentation
`Stochastic_Option_Pricing_Presentation_v2.pptx`.  Use it as a reference
while rehearsing; every important formula is here with short comments and
pointers to the literature.

I only cover Black–Scholes (BS) and Merton Jump–Diffusion (MJD), plus model
selection. Heston/Bates are mentioned only in a brief future‑work note.

Notation:
  S(t)      – underlying index level (NIFTY 50)
  μ, σ      – physical‑measure drift and volatility
  r_f       – risk‑free rate (RBI repo, continuous compounding)
  W(t)      – standard Brownian motion
  N(t)      – Poisson process with intensity λ (jumps/year)
  J         – log jump size,  J ~ N(μ_J, σ_J²)
  Δt        – time step for daily data = 1/252
  r_t       – log‑return ln(S_t / S_{t-1})

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Black–Scholes model
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 GBM SDE and log‑price solution
----------------------------------
Assume under the physical measure P:

  dS(t) = μ S(t) dt + σ S(t) dW(t)                                       (1)

Apply Itô’s Lemma to f(S) = ln S:

  df = f_S dS + ½ f_SS (dS)²
     = (1/S) dS − ½ (1/S²) (σ² S² dt)                                   (2)

Substituting dS from (1):

  d(ln S(t)) = (μ − ½ σ²) dt + σ dW(t)                                  (3)

Integrating from 0 to T:

  ln S(T) = ln S(0) + (μ − ½ σ²) T + σ W(T)
  ⇒ S(T)  = S(0) · exp{ (μ − ½ σ²)T + σ√T Z },   Z ~ N(0,1)             (4)

This is what you show on the “BS theory” slide; it explains why S(T) is
log‑normal and why log‑returns are Gaussian.


1.2 Daily log‑returns
---------------------
Let Δt = 1/252 and consider S_t = S(tΔt). From (3),

  r_t := ln(S_t / S_{t-1}) = (μ − ½ σ²)Δt + σ √Δt Z_t,   Z_t ~ N(0,1)   (5)

Hence

  r_t ~ N( (μ − ½ σ²)Δt, σ² Δt ).

This is the likelihood model used for MLE on NIFTY log‑returns.


1.3 MLE for (μ, σ)
------------------
Given returns r_1,…,r_n, independent N(m, v) with
  m = (μ − ½ σ²)Δt,  v = σ² Δt,
the log‑likelihood is

  ℓ(μ, σ)
    = − (n/2) ln(2π) − (n/2) ln v
      − ∑_{i=1}^n (r_i − m)² / (2 v).                                   (6)

A convenient reparameterisation is
  μ̃ := μ − ½ σ² ⇒ m = μ̃ Δt.

Taking derivatives and solving gives the closed‑form MLE:

  σ̂² = (1/(n Δt)) ∑_{i=1}^n (r_i − r̄)²,
  μ̂  = r̄ / Δt + ½ σ̂²,                                                 (7)

where r̄ is the sample mean of returns.  In the code we *numerically*
maximise ℓ(μ,σ) with Nelder–Mead instead of plugging in (7) directly, so
that the optimisation framework can be reused for MJD.

Key points to say orally:
  • For GBM the MLE has a closed form, but we still use numerical
    optimisation to keep the pipeline consistent.
  • The estimate σ̂ from NIFTY data is about 17–18% annualised.

Reference: Aït‑Sahalia (2002), Econometrica 70(1), for MLE of diffusions.


1.4 Risk‑neutral measure and pricing
------------------------------------
Under the risk‑neutral measure Q, the drift becomes r_f:

  dS(t) = r_f S(t) dt + σ S(t) dW^Q(t).                                 (8)

Arbitrage‑free price of a derivative with payoff Φ(S(T)):

  V(0) = e^{−r_f T} · E^Q[ Φ(S(T)) ].                                   (9)

For a European call Φ(S(T)) = max(S(T) − K, 0), explicit integration
gives Black–Scholes:

  C = S_0 Φ(d₁) − K e^{−r_f T} Φ(d₂),                                   (10)
  d₁ = [ln(S_0/K) + (r_f + ½ σ²)T] / (σ√T),
  d₂ = d₁ − σ√T.

You can always stress that the **same σ̂_MLE from historical data** is
plugged into this formula.


1.5 Monte Carlo estimator
-------------------------
Discretise (8) on n steps of size Δt = T/n:

  ln S_{k+1} = ln S_k + (r_f − ½ σ²)Δt + σ√Δt Z_k.

Simulate N paths, compute discounted payoffs:

  Ĉ_MC = e^{−r_f T} (1/N) ∑ max(S_T^{(i)} − K, 0).                       (11)

By the CLT the standard error shrinks ∝ 1/√N. The convergence plot in the
slides shows Ĉ_MC → C_BS.

Reference: Glasserman (2003), *Monte Carlo Methods in Financial
Engineering*.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Merton Jump–Diffusion model
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 SDE and jump structure
--------------------------
Physical‑measure SDE:

  dS(t) = μ S(t) dt + σ S(t) dW(t) + S(t−)(e^J − 1) dN(t),               (12)

where:
  • N(t) is a Poisson process with intensity λ (expected jumps per year),
  • J is the log jump size,  J ~ N(μ_J, σ_J²), i.i.d. across jumps,
  • W, N, and J’s are mutually independent.

Intuition:
  – GBM handles “small, continuous” moves;
  – Poisson jumps handle sudden dislocations (crashes, gap moves).


2.2 Log‑price dynamics via Itô for jump‑diffusions
--------------------------------------------------
For f(S) = ln S, Itô’s formula for jump‑diffusions gives

  d(ln S(t))
    = (μ − ½ σ²) dt + σ dW(t) + J dN(t).                                (13)

Over a small time step Δt with N jumps equal to k, we integrate:

  r := ln(S_{t+Δt} / S_t)
      = (μ − ½ σ²)Δt + σ√Δt Z + ∑_{j=1}^k J_j.                          (14)

Conditionally on N=k:
  r | N=k ~ N( (μ − ½ σ²)Δt + k μ_J,   σ² Δt + k σ_J² ).               (15)


2.3 Mixture likelihood and unconditional density
------------------------------------------------
Unconditional density for r is a Poisson‑weighted mixture of Gaussians:

  f(r)
    = ∑_{k=0}^∞ P(N=k) · φ(r; m_k, v_k)                                 (16)
    = ∑_{k=0}^∞ e^{−λΔt}(λΔt)^k/k! · φ(r; m_k, v_k),

where
  m_k = (μ − ½ σ²)Δt + k μ_J,
  v_k = σ² Δt + k σ_J²,
  φ(·;m,v) is the N(m,v) density.

In practice we truncate the sum at k = 15–20; for realistic λ this is
enough since higher‑k terms have negligible weight.

Given returns r₁,…,r_n, the log‑likelihood is

  ℓ(θ) = ∑_{t=1}^n ln[ ∑_{k=0}^K e^{−λΔt}(λΔt)^k/k! · φ(r_t; m_k, v_k) ],
                                                                     (17)
with θ = (μ, σ, λ, μ_J, σ_J).  We maximise ℓ numerically with L‑BFGS‑B
subject to natural constraints σ>0, λ≥0, σ_J>0.

Key things to mention:
  • The inner sum is the Gaussian mixture; we evaluate it for **every**
    daily return in the sample ⇒ computationally heavy.
  • The likelihood surface is higher‑dimensional, so we need a robust
    optimiser with bounds (L‑BFGS‑B).

The code uses a fully vectorised implementation to make this feasible.


2.4 Moments and fat tails
-------------------------
Unconditional variance of r:

  Var(r) = σ² Δt + λ Δt (μ_J² + σ_J²).                                  (18)

The second term is the jump contribution: it is zero under BS (λ=0) and
positive under MJD, giving extra variability and kurtosis.

An analytical expression for excess kurtosis is

  Kurt_excess(MJD)
    = 3 λ (μ_J² + σ_J²)² / [ σ² + λ(μ_J² + σ_J²) ]².                    (19)

This is the quantity you quote on the “kurtosis table” slide.  For NIFTY
data, empirical excess kurtosis ≈ 22, BS gives 0 by construction, MJD
can attain values in the same ball‑park depending on the estimated λ,
μ_J, σ_J.


2.5 Risk‑neutral drift and pricing
----------------------------------
Define
  κ := E[e^J − 1] = exp(μ_J + ½ σ_J²) − 1.                              (20)

Under Q, the compensated SDE is

  dS(t) = r_f S(t) dt + σ S(t) dW^Q(t) + S(t−)(e^J − 1)(dN(t) − λ κ dt),
                                                                     (21)
so that E^Q[S(t)] = S(0) e^{r_f t}.  This is the “no‑arbitrage drift
adjustment” line on the slide.

In practice we use Monte Carlo under Q to price options, with the
compensated drift μ_rn = r_f − ½ σ² − λ κ.


2.6 Likelihood‑ratio test and information criteria
--------------------------------------------------
Black–Scholes is nested in MJD when λ → 0 and μ_J=0, σ_J=0.  Under the
null H₀: “no jumps”, the likelihood‑ratio statistic

  Λ = 2 [ ℓ_MJD − ℓ_BS ]  ~  χ²(3).                                     (22)

The 3 degrees of freedom correspond to the additional parameters λ,
μ_J, σ_J.  A very large Λ with p‑value ≈ 0 means the data strongly
prefer MJD.

On NIFTY data you obtained Λ ≈ 571 with p ≈ 0, which is **overwhelming**
evidence in favour of jumps.

Information criteria:

  AIC = 2k − 2ℓ(θ̂),
  BIC = k ln n − 2ℓ(θ̂),                                                (23)

with k parameters and n observations.  Despite k_MJD = 5 vs k_BS = 2,
the gain in log‑likelihood is so large that AIC and BIC are both lower
for MJD, confirming that the extra parameters are justified.

References:
  – Merton (1976), J. Financial Economics 3(1–2).
  – Aït‑Sahalia & Jacod (2007), Annals of Statistics 35(1).
  – Cont & Tankov (2004), *Financial Modelling with Jump Processes*.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 Return distribution vs Normal (Figure 1 bottom‑left)
--------------------------------------------------------
Explain:
  • Histogram of NIFTY daily log‑returns over 2018–2024.
  • Red curve is the Gaussian N(m̂, ŝ²) using sample mean and s.d.

  – Under BS, r_t is exactly Gaussian ⇒ kurtosis 0.
  – Empirical kurtosis ≈ 22 ⇒ incompatible with BS assumptions.

-------------------------------------------------
Here you focus only on returns below −1%.  Emphasise that:
  • The histogram bars in the left tail are much higher than the red
    normal curve.
  • Vertical lines at 1% and 0.5% empirical VaR show where BS assigns
    almost zero probability.

Link back to equations (18)–(19): adding the jump term λ(μ_J²+σ_J²)
inflates tail probabilities precisely in this region.


3.3 GBM vs MJD paths (Figure 3)
-------------------------------
Use the new four‑panel figure:
  • Top‑left: GBM paths — smooth, continuous, no sudden jumps.
  • Top‑right: MJD paths — red vertical lines and dots marking jump

  • Bottom‑left: same random seed for diffusion with/without jumps;

This helps answer the “I don’t see jumps” confusion — the estimated

3.4 Tail behaviour and QQ plots
-------------------------------
Figures 4 and 5 compare:
  • BS simulated returns vs empirical vs MJD simulated returns.
  • In QQ plots, tails of empirical distribution deviate strongly from
    the 45° line under BS, but much less under MJD.

Relate to excess kurtosis formula (19) and variance decomposition (18).


3.5 VaR backtesting
-------------------
Explain that BS 1% VaR systematically underestimates losses — you see
too many exceedances (red dots) and cumulative breaches outside the
Kupiec 95% band.  The empirical VaR (proxy for MJD) tracks the target
1% rate much better.

4. Future work: Heston / Bates (for you only)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In the PPT you now just have a short “Future work” bullet list.  If asked
verbally, you can say:

  • Heston will relax **constant volatility** by modelling v(t) as a CIR
    process, d v = κ(θ − v) dt + ξ √v dW_v.
  • Bates will add jumps on top of Heston, combining stochastic volatility
    and jumps to match the full IV surface.
  • Numerical simulation for Heston’s √v(t) term is delicate (can go
    negative); this is where Prof. Kumar’s tamed Euler / Milstein schemes
    are highly relevant for stable Monte Carlo pricing.

You don’t need to derive Heston/Bates for this mid‑term; just mention
them as natural next steps.
